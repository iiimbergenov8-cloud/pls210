PLS 210 — Research Design, Part 1

Does Part-Time Work Help Graduates Land High-Paid Jobs?

Skills or networks? A positivist inquiry into Kazakhstani graduates' first jobs.

ozindi_ayama · Presentation #1

Research question

Does part-time work during studies increase Kazakhstani university graduates' chances of getting a high-paid first job, and is this because of skills gained or because of networks made at work?
  • Population: university graduates in Kazakhstan; first job within 12 months of graduation
  • Part-time work: paid work alongside full-time study (yes/no, weekly hours, field relevance)
  • High-paid: starting monthly salary above the sample median, collected in brackets

Positioning: positivist

  • Pay and job-finding are observable and measurable.
  • I look for a general pattern across many graduates, not one person's story.
  • Two competing, falsifiable hypotheses, following King, Keohane & Verba (1994): theory as a testable answer to a question.
Positionality: I worked in sales for 2+ years alongside my studies. I may expect work to pay off, so I test two explanations against each other and report results even if they surprise me.

Contribution

  • Student-work research comes mostly from the US, Australia and China, and mostly measures grades.
  • Almost none covers Kazakhstan, where informal hiring and uneven pay by city and field may change what work experience is worth.
  • New here: testing skills against networks, rather than assuming one reason, with starting pay as a concrete outcome.

Literature: skills and networks

  • Becker (1964): experience builds skills, which raise productivity and pay.
  • Van Belle et al. (2020): 242 employers; student work raises hiring odds, but only when field-relevant.
  • Jackson (2023): 150,000+ Australian graduates; work experience built into the degree gave clear labor-market gains.
  • Granovetter (1973): people often find jobs through contacts, not formal applications.

Literature: the gap and the local context

  • Fei, Ghazali & Tham (2025): 750 Chinese students; skills and experience predict employment, but networks are not tested. The gap.
  • HeadHunter KZ (2013): 67% combined work and study. Self-reported and dated, so background only.
  • Poplavskaya et al. (2026): 2,723 Russian undergraduates; studies grades, not pay. A regional comparator.
  • Bluescreen.kz (2026): 43.6% of young Kazakhstanis work or combine study and work. Journalistic, context only.

Theory: two paths to a high-paid job

Hypotheses and how to tell them apart

H1: skills

Work builds practical skills that employers reward, even for graduates hired by open application.

Measure: self-rated skills gained at work.

H2: networks

Work builds contacts and referrals, so the benefit appears mainly among referral hires.

Measure: how the first job was found.

Controls: field, city, year of study, job relevance. Limit: self-selection, so the result shows a pattern, not proof of causation.
